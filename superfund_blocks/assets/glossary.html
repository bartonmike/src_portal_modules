Project Overview

The Oregon State University - Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (OSU-PNNL) Superfund Center focuses on polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).   The Superfund Research Program (SRP), part of the   National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS), supports university-based research centers that investigate the environmental and health effects of hazardous substances. PAHs are a group of hundreds of different chemicals formed by burning organic material.

PAHs are a health concern because people can be exposed to them through contaminated air, water, soil, and tobacco smoke. Some PAHs are known or suspected carcinogens, while others are associated with adverse health effects such as respiratory disease, impaired immune function, reproductive, and developmental outcomes.

This website brings together data and visualizations from the OSU-PNNL research program. Passive samplers provide information about environmental and personal exposure profiles. Zebrafish assays quantify developmental and behavioral changes following controlled chemical exposures to detect biological anomalies, measurements of the expression in genes in response to chemicals.

Understanding bmdrc

Models

Dose–response models are fitted to describe the relationship between chemical concentration and the observed response in zebrafish. The available models depend on whether the endpoint is measured as a proportional response or as a continuous response.

Proportional-Response Models

Used when the response is measured as a proportion or percentage, such as the percentage of zebrafish affected at each concentration.

  • Logistic
  • Gamma
  • Weibull
  • Log-Logistic
  • Probit
  • Log-Probit
  • Multistage
  • Quantal Linear

Continuous-Response Models

Used when the response is measured on a continuous scale, such as movement, length, weight, or another numerical measurement.

  • Asymptotic Regression
  • Exponential
  • Gompertz
  • Hill
  • Michaelis-Menten
  • Power
  • Weibull
Note: See the glossary for definitions and additional details about each dose–response model.

Model Selection Workflow

Each endpoint is evaluated using an automated process to identify the best-fitting dose–response model.

Process

Description

Fit Models

Candidate models are fit to proportional or continuous-response data. The available models depend on the endpoint type.

Check Fit

Models with a goodness-of-fit p-value below 0.1 are removed from consideration.

Compare AIC

Models within 2 AIC units of the lowest AIC value are retained. Lower AIC values indicate a better balance between model fit and complexity.

Select Model

The retained model with the lowest BMDL is selected, consistent with EPA guidance.

Morphology Endpoints

Morphological endpoints capture physical abnormalities in developing zebrafish. Each phenotype is scored as absent or present and may be modeled as the proportion of affected fish within a treatment group.

During processing, individual phenotypes may be combined into aggregate endpoints. Examples include:

  • Cranium: An aggregate endpoint that includes abnormalities of the eye, snout, and jaw.
  • Total Mortality (24 hours): Mortality recorded 24 hours after exposure.
  • Total Mortality (5 days): Mortality recorded 5 days after exposure.

See the Zebrafish Endpoints table for descriptions of all morphological endpoints.

Behavioral Endpoints (LPR)

Larval photomotor response (LPR) measures zebrafish movement during alternating light and dark cycles. These measurements are treated as continuous-response data.

To process LPR data, the light/dark exposure-cycle length, time between cycles, and whether the light or dark cycle occurs first are specified. These settings are used to derive behavioral endpoints.

Endpoint

Description

AUC

5 days total movement. This endpoint summarizes the total amount of movement measured during the behavioral assay.

MOV

Behavioral transition. This endpoint describes the change in movement between light and dark cycles.

LPR input data is stored in long format and include the chemical identifier, concentration, plate identifier, well, measured movement, and measurement time.

Data Types

Zebrafish

Zebrafish are small tropical freshwater vertebrates widely used as a research model because they share many conserved genes, organs, and developmental pathways with humans. Their rapid, transparent early development and high rates of reproduction enable in vivo studies of chemical and mixture toxicity across developmental, behavioral, and molecular endpoints.

Endpoints

An “endpoint” is a measurement of a phenotype in response to increasing concentrations of a chemical. Examples of endpoints include physiological changes in zebrafish anatomy such as malformations of the cranium, behavioral changes in zebrafish, such as fry failing to respond to light stimulus, and responses in cellular studies (such as mitochondrial membrane potential). This website includes the following endpoints:

Environmental Samplers

Passive sampling devices (PSDs) absorb organic contaminants from the environment during their deployment. Because these devices collect chemicals continuously, they can provide a measurement that reflects the average contaminant level during a specific period. PSDs are placed in air, water, and sediment near Superfund sites to measure environmental chemical exposure.

Passive Sampling Devices

Air sampler

Sampling material is housed inside a protective device that allows air and airborne chemicals to flow through and contact the sampler.

Water Sampler

Sampling material is arranged on metal disks inside a perforated cylinder, allowing water and dissolved chemicals to flow through and contact the sampler.

Sediment Sampler

The sampler is buried in the sediment so water can flow through the holes in the metal frame and carry dissolved chemicals into the sampling material.

Wristbands

Made of silicone, this sampler is worn like a bracelet and detects chemicals to which the wearer is exposed.

A Note on Passive Sample Data

Samples from Superfund sites have also been administered to zebrafish, and the results can be found on the   "Environmental Samples" pages with zebrafish data.

Human Exposome

Overview

Wristbands measure personal exposure to chemicals continuously throughout their deployment, helping to characterize exposure profiles rather than exposure to single compounds. These measurements support research on the potential health impacts of those exposures. Because wristbands are lightweight, easy to transport, and require minimal maintenance, they offer a unique opportunity to assess chemical exposure in communities that may be difficult to reach with traditional assessment methods, including remote populations and vulnerable groups during disruptive situations such as disasters or pandemics. Wristbands can also simultaneously capture many chemicals, enabling mixture focused analyses and prioritization of chemical combinations for future studies. The results should be interpreted as external exposures as opposed to internal doses such as chemicals in the bloodstream.

How It Works

  • Wear: Participants wear a silicone wristband during daily life.
  • Passive uptake: Chemicals move from the surrounding environment into the silicone over time.
  • Return and laboratory analysis: Wristbands are easy to wear, generally require minimal training and can be returned to the laboratory by mail at ambient temperature for extraction and chemical analysis.

Understanding the Zebrafish Morphological Measurements Table

This table summarizes dose–response modeling results for zebrafish morphological and behavioral endpoints. Each row represents a measured biological endpoint, such as Any Effect, Craniofacial, or Brain. Select an endpoint name to view its corresponding concentration–response curve.

The remaining columns provide a summary of the fitted dose–response measurements. Descriptions of each column are provided below.

Columns

Description

Endpoint

Morphological and behavioral endpoints. Select an underlined endpoint (e.g., Any effect) name to display the corresponding concentration–response curve plot

AUC (Area Under the Curve)

A unitless summary, ranging from 0 to 1, of the overall response magnitude across the tested concentration range.

0 indicates a flat line at 0% response, meaning no response was observed at any concentration.

1 indicates a flat line at 100% response, meaning the maximum response was observed at all concentrations.

**Higher AUC values indicate a stronger or broader response for that endpoint**

BMD10 (Benchmark Dose, 10%)

The estimated concentration at which 10% of zebrafish are affected. Often used to identify an earlier or lower-level effect.

A lower BMD10 value indicates that an effect occurs at lower concentrations.

BMD50 (Benchmark Dose, 50%)

The estimated concentration at which 50% of zebrafish are affected. Represents a larger, more common effect in the tested population.

A lower BMD50 means half of the zebrafish are affected at a lower concentration.

Feature

Description

The tested chemical concentration or dilution level. Values increase from left to right.

For morphological endpoints, the y-axis generally represents the percentage of zebrafish affected, from 0% to 100%.

For behavioral or continuous-response endpoints, the y-axis may represent an average measurement, such as movement, rather than the percentage of zebrafish affected.

The plotted points represent the observed response at each tested concentration.

Vertical lines, when shown, represent variation or uncertainty among replicate measurements.

Fitted Curve

The fitted curve is a mathematical model that describes the relationship between chemical concentration and the measured response.

The curve is used to calculate summary measurements such as AUC, BMD10, and BMD50.

Model

The model name identifies the dose–response model selected as the best fit for the observed data. Models may include logistic, log-logistic, Hill, exponential, polynomial, or other model types.

Header Information

The plot header identifies the selected endpoint, the fitted model, and applicable model summary measurements, including AUC, BMD10, and BMD50.

How to Interpret the Curve Shape

Curve Shape

Interpretation

Steep Rise

The response changes rapidly over a narrow range of concentrations.

Gradual Rise

The response increases more slowly across the tested concentration range.

Flat Near 0%

Little or no response was observed across the tested concentrations.

Plateau Near 100%

The maximum proportional response has been reached. Increasing the concentration further may not substantially increase the percentage of zebrafish affected.

Glossary

This glossary defines terms used throughout the website, including interactive charts, environmental exposure, zebrafish endpoints, and dose–response modeling.

Website and Chart Terms

  • Observed Data Points: Measurements collected experimentally at each tested chemical concentration. These measurements are displayed as points on a chart.
  • Fitted Curve: A mathematical curve fitted to the observed data. It describes the estimated relationship between chemical concentration and the measured response.
  • Hover: Moving the pointer over a chart element, such as a bar, point, or line, to view its label and exact value.

Environmental Exposure and Sampling

  • Extract: The solution produced when chemicals are removed from sampler material, such as silicone, using solvents. The extract is analyzed by an instrument.
  • Analyte: An individual chemical compound measured in an environmental or biological sample.
  • External Exposure: Contact with chemicals in the surrounding environment. External exposure is different from internal dose, which refers to the amount of a chemical present inside an organism.

Zebrafish and Biological Endpoints

  • Larval Photomotor Response (LPR): A behavioral assay that measures zebrafish movement during alternating light and dark cycles.

Dose–Response and Model Terms

  • Logistic: Uses the logistic function to estimate the probability of a binary response, which ranges from 0 to 1. It commonly produces an S-shaped concentration–response curve.
  • Gamma: Uses the gamma distribution to model positive, continuous, and often right-skewed responses. It is generally used when the variability of the response increases with its magnitude.
  • Log-Logistic: Uses a logistic model with the logarithm of dose or concentration as the predictor.
  • Probit: Uses the cumulative standard normal distribution to estimate the probability of a binary response. The predicted probability ranges from 0 to 1.
  • Log-Probit: Uses a probit model with the logarithm of dose or concentration as the predictor.
  • Multistage: Uses a polynomial function of dose to represent the probability of a response. The model can include linear and higher-order dose terms.
  • Quantal Linear: Uses a linear dose-related component to model the probability of a quantal response.
  • Weibull: Uses a continuous probability distribution to model how the probability of a response changes as chemical concentration increases.
  • Asymptotic Regression: Used when a function changes gradually and approaches a fixed upper or lower limit, known as an asymptote.
  • Exponential: Models a response that increases or decreases at a rate proportional to its current value.
  • Gompertz: Used to model growth that starts slowly, changes most rapidly in the middle of the concentration range, and then slows again as it approaches a limit, often forming an asymmetric S-shaped curve.
  • Power: Models the measured response as a power function of dose or concentration. The power parameter determines whether the response increases at a decreasing rate, linearly, or at an increasing rate.
  • Hill: Models a response that approaches a maximum or minimum as dose or concentration increases.
  • Michaelis–Menten: Uses a saturation function to model a response that approaches a maximum or minimum as concentration increases.
  • Goodness-of-Fit p-Value: A statistical value used to evaluate how well a model describes the observed data. In the model-selection workflow, models with a p-value below 0.1 may be removed from consideration.
  • AIC (Akaike Information Criterion): A statistic used to compare models while considering both model fit and complexity. Lower AIC values generally indicate a better balance between fit and complexity.
  • NA (Not Available): Indicates that a model result or metric could not be estimated reliably for a particular endpoint.
  • Model Selection: The process of comparing candidate dose–response models and selecting the model that best meets the fitting and evaluation criteria.

Units and Measurements

  • ng/L (Nanograms per Liter): One billionth of a gram per liter.
  • pmol/mL (Picomoles per Milliliter): One trillionth of a mole per milliliter.
  • Percentage Affected: The percentage of organisms or samples showing a defined biological response at a particular chemical concentration.
  • Concentration: The amount of a chemical present in a specified volume or mass of a sample, solution, or exposure medium.
